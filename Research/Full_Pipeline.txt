Phase 2: Create "Ground Truth" Labels (the hardest, most time-consuming part)

This is where you teach the AI what water actually looks like, by hand.

For a subset of your images (your reference doc caps this at 300-500 small patches, not all 24 full images), you'll manually trace the water boundary in QGIS.
Practically: you don't label the whole big image at once — you cut each image into smaller patches (e.g., 256×256 pixel tiles), and manually draw/correct a water-vs-land mask on each patch.
You'll use NDWI/MNDWI (computed from your NIR/SWIR bands) as a first-pass automatic guess, then manually fix mistakes in QGIS — especially algae patches in Hussain Sagar, which the auto-index gets wrong.
Output of this phase: pairs of (image patch, correct water/land mask) — this is your training data.

Phase 3: Train the U-Net Model
Feed your labeled patches into a U-Net (Python, PyTorch, using the segmentation-models-pytorch library with a pretrained ResNet34 encoder — per your tech stack).
The model learns: "given these 5 bands of pixel data, predict water vs. land for every pixel."
You'll split your labeled patches into training/validation sets, train for some epochs, and check accuracy.

Phase 4: Run the Trained Model on All 24 Images
Once trained, the model doesn't need manual labels anymore — it automatically predicts a water/land mask for every one of your 24 full images (including any images you didn't manually label).
Output: 24 predicted masks — one per lake per year.

Phase 5: Convert Masks → Surface Area Numbers
For each predicted mask, count the water pixels and multiply by pixel area (10m × 10m = 100 m² per pixel) to get total surface area in km².
Now you have a simple table: Lake × Year × Surface Area (km²).

Phase 6: Analyze & Report the Trend
